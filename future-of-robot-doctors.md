# A Brief History of a Machine

Author's Note: This is a work of fiction. Any resemblance to real persons, living or dead, is purely coincidental. This was written for my college Science Fiction class, and I hope you enjoy it.

Everything reminded Jocko of his last tour in Afghanistan. The hard, metal benches
prodded his tailbone, sneaking past any cushion his body offered. His hefty vest was unbalanced,
compelling him to lean forward. The assault rifle in his hands grew increasingly heavy as the
flight wore on, making his forearms ache. No, he thought to himself. That’s not right. This isn’t a
flight. This is a car ride. And I’m not in Afghanistan, I’m in Pennsylvania.

To reassure himself, he glanced to his left and right. His eyes did not detect any Army
soldiers, only men with the same “All About Security Agency” patch decorating the left arm of
an otherwise black uniform. Many of them, like himself, were veterans with multiple
deployments under their belts. Their employer had not spared any expense when putting together
this private security detail.

But the easiest way for Jocko to ground himself was not by looking at the people. One
glance at the machine in the middle of the room confirmed he was no longer overseas. It was the
antithesis of any tank, HumV, or bomb Jocko had ever encountered; it was only the second time
he'd seen one. Even in the midnight blackness of a country road, whatever trace light touched it
was immediately reflected, allowing Jocko to see the elegant curves and sharp lines that
culminated to form the most famous machine in the world.

His introduction to the machine was how many people first encountered it: in a hospital.
A helicopter accident left him injured, and he had been told a machine named Watson would be
conducting his surgery. There would still be people in the room, the nurse assured him, since
people want to trust another person over a machine.

Like the millions of other surgeries Watson machines had performed across the world,
Jocko’s went perfectly. The doubters of robot-doctors were soon silenced by the effectiveness of the machines in both the physical and mental arenas of healthcare: for example, Watson detects
lung cancer 90% of the time successfully, compared to the human rate of 50% (Steadman). That,
paired with its ability to read and analyze scientific studies from around the world,
communicating with other Watson doctors, left the need for human doctors obsolete.

The robot takeover that many feared never occurred. Instead, people worked alongside
Watson, supplying the indispensable need for human connection, especially concerning one’s
health. Watson seemed symbiotic in its relation to humans—until politics became involved.

Watson was designed to perform surgeries—all surgeries. In states that outlawed
abortion, people were able to steal Watsons and order them to conduct the surgery. Being a loyal
robot, it obliged. Suddenly, Watson was breaking the law. In Georgia, a Watson robot had
actually been put on trial. It sat on the witness stand, telling the complete truth and nothing but
the truth. Before he was short-circuited, his final words were for the prosecutor to get his
pancreas checked.

The trial forced JCN, the designer of Watson, to create two editions, one without the
ability to perform abortions coupled with an integrated shielding in their circuitry to prevent
them from learning. But many people still thought of this robot as a baby-killing machine and
felt a moral duty to stop this murder. Getting no help from the courts, which ruled against them
citing state independence, they took the matter into their own hands, finding and disabling
manufacturing plants, R&D offices, and distribution trucks. The CEO’s house had recently been
vandalized with the slogan “Baby Killer.”

The route from Pittsburg, JCN’s largest functioning manufacturing plant, to New York,
was one of the most common routes for new Watson machines to take. The hub of New York allowed Watson to be distributed across the globe, into the farthest reaches of the Earth where it
was needed the most. However, the path was also the most dangerous, crossing through many
pro-life communities in a state with no assault weapon restrictions and no requirement for a
permit in order to buy a gun (Higgins). Jocko had been on this route many times, but tonight
seemed different. Looking at Watson #7592859, he thought that machine looked poised,
somehow ready for something.

Bringing himself out of his thoughts, he glanced around to see if anyone had seen him
daydreaming. The man to his left, Reggie, sat as stout as a statue, prompting Jocko to change
posture from the Thinker to the Buddha.

A sudden crack in the night startled him. All of his muscles tensed, forcing him into a
squat just above the bench. He glared, wondering who on this detail had accidentally fired a
round. The answer came in the form of a whisper of wind on his neck that his senses barely
registered. Rapidly pivoting to the right, he discovered the source of the wind was a hole about
the size of a quarter in the 18-wheeler’s shell. How did his adjacent soldier manage to fire a
round that exited the truck behind his head?

The realization came too late. The rest of the company was already on the ground,
shouting for backup into their radios. Bruising his elbows to take cover, Jocko found himself
entranced by the machine that had risen as the soldiers had fallen. Watson had picked up Jocko’s
fallen neighbor, and, realizing the futility in trying to save a headshot wound, injected morphine
into his system. Jocko tore his entranced eyes away, exiting the death box along with the rest of
the company. They hid behind the truck, firing shots into the darkness as bullets whizzed past them. His eyes could not see anything in the dim light, but he shot where his ears guessed the
assailant’s were.

After a deafening thirty seconds, snakes stopped whizzing through the air. Jocko first
examined himself, ensuring that his adrenaline did not disguise any wounds from his
consciousness. Satisfied, he scanned his troop. Reggie was a couple feet away from him, looking
at the stars while a midnight black stream poured out of his left thigh.

“Hey Watson!” yelled Jocko, frantically. “We need your help. Reggie’s bleeding out!”
Emerging from the shadows, Watson glanced toward the soldier unit assigned to protect him, and
then it moved in the other direction.

“Hey!” Jocko cried desperately. “Come back! My friend needs your help!” But Watson
either did not hear or did not adhere, for it was crouched over a shadow in the dark, instruments
whirring in the darkness. Finally, the machine made its way to Reggie, efficiently cauterizing and
bandaging the wound.

“Why, the fuck, did you go help those murderers?” asked Jocko, aggressively.
“The patient there needed more immediate attention because his wound was more
critical,” responded Watson, robotically.

“They were trying to kill you, to demolish you! Don’t you understand that? Don’t you
have an ounce of self-preservation? They were trying to prevent you from helping people!”
Watson responded only with a phrase, as if it explained it all: “Do no harm.”
