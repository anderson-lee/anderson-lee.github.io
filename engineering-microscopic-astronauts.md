# Engineering microscopic astronauts

    ![Alt text](/images/mars.jpg)

On February 18th, 2021, NASA’s rover, “Perseverance,” and its mini-helicopter, “Ingenuity,”
landed in the Jezero crater on the surface of Mars. Percy and Ginny, as they are colloquially
called, are the most advanced robots ever sent to Mars. Soon, however, we will be sending
something less durable, more variable, and much more valuable: humans.

Supporting human life inherently makes this mission more difficult: people need food, water, and
oxygen. The waste produced—carbon dioxide and physical excrements—must then be recycled
or discarded into the environment.

The long-term viability of humans in space also raises concerns. Exposure to radiation and
zero-gravity environments will alter cellular DNA and break down tissue, posing a myriad of
health problems during a multi-year mission. Bringing medicine along is problematic because
many drugs will expire before return to Earth.

In order to address these concerns caused by biology, NASA has started to employ biological
technologies. When mixing the realms of space travel and biology, The Martian and Matt

Damon’s potatoes tend to be on the forefront of people’s minds. Plants, however, require room to
grow, soil, and water, and most of the biomass produced is either nutrient sparse or inedible.
Because of this, scientists are now focusing on edible bacteria. Many species of bacteria (and
other microorganisms) produce biomass full of proteins and vitamins.<sup>1</sup> These bacteria can grow
in small areas and are relatively simple to genetically engineer. Scientists are engineering
bacteria in new ways to combat the unique challenges of space travel.

Scientists employ a technique called metabolic engineering in order to optimize bacteria to meet
the demands of space travel. Metabolism in plants and bacteria is similar to that in humans:
generally, they break down food and modify it to make energy. This process is a pathway of
sequential steps, each step comprising an enzyme transforming a molecule into something else.
The result of these steps produces byproducts in addition to energy, and it is these byproducts
that are potentially useful.

Different enzymes can bind to the same molecule and produce different results. Researchers can
genetically engineer bacteria so that specific enzymes are present or absent, changing the final
product of metabolism. This is when scientists can start using their creativity: from producing
essential vitamins to making plants taste better, the possibilities of metabolic engineering are
only limited by our knowledge of nature and our ability to manipulate it.

The Center for Utilization of Biological Engineering in Space (CUBES) showcased their creative
ideas in a review focusing on the biological production of useful compounds. Acetaminophen,
the active ingredient in Tylenol, is one such example. Producing medicines like acetaminophen
on demand, using metabolic engineering, is critical for long duration missions because their shelf
life is shorter than the length of the trip.<sup>2</sup>

The production of therapeutics by organisms, known as molecular pharming, could also include
cytokines, molecules that the immune system uses to communicate. Astronauts could eat the
bacteria containing these molecules to counter the short-term effects of radiation sickness and the
long-term effects of cancer.<sup>3</sup>

Bacterial production in space extends beyond astronaut health—photosynthesizing bacteria could
uptake the carbon dioxide produced by humans and recycle it into breathable oxygen. They
could also be metabolically engineered to grow off of the compounds present in the Martian soil
or human waste, creating a closed-loop life support system.<sup>4</sup>

The disparity between theoretical plausibility and dependable technology, however, spans many
years into the future. So far, the vast majority of metabolic engineering has been focused on
creating biofuels to address the need for alternative energy.<sup>5</sup>

A select few model organisms have been extensively characterized in the interest of biofuel production, but scientists can use the already acquired knowledge and utilize it in the context of human space travel. This application
makes photosynthetic and edible organisms valuable so that they can be grown easily and their
products don’t have to be extracted.

In 2012, molecular pharming became a reality when a company received FDA authorization to
sell a therapeutic protein that was made in plants.<sup>6</sup> This marks the first biologically-produced
medicine with FDA approval, demonstrating molecular pharming’s plausibility here on Earth and
out in space.

The challenges facing molecular pharming and metabolic engineering in general are undoubtedly
more intimidating in space travel than on Earth. When an organism is genetically edited to make
a product, its priority is to survive, and whatever scientists are interested in is a byproduct. In
space, these organisms will likely be under stress due to the different environmental conditions,
like the lack of gravity. The yield of a product might be lower than that on Earth, making it a
waste of precious energy.

Additionally, the power of replication is coupled with the problem of mutation. If a mutation
causes a critical enzyme to stop working, that cell will no longer be able to produce the desired
product. On top of that, this cell will not experience the metabolic burden of producing its
biologic, so it could out-compete the other, non-mutated cells and take over the culture. Other
challenges include simply keeping the bacteria alive during the arduous trip—something that is
currently being investigated on the International Space Station.<sup>7</sup>

If all of these problems are addressed, what would this system look like on a Mars trip? Ideally,
cells would be genetically engineered, frozen, and stored in small, labeled tubes. When needed, a
tube could be removed, thawed, and grown up into a large culture that produces significant
quantities of its biologic. Until this technology is proven, though, preliminary missions could
harbor these engineered cells as a worst-case scenario, a Matt-Damon-stranded-on-Mars type of
scenario.

This technology will likely not be exclusive to spaceships and research labs. Scientific research
meant for space travel has already infiltrated normal life, including innovations like tempurpedic
mattresses, cell phone cameras, and LASIK, among others.<sup>8</sup> One day, people might be able to
grow Tylenol next to their tomato plants.

The future of human space travel lies within the powers of biology; the natural problems that
organisms pose have solutions found in nature. The terraformers of Mars will be a mix of
astronauts—both human-sized and microscopic—working together to start exploring the
universe.
